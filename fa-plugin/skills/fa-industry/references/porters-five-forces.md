# Porter's Five Forces — Adapted for Indian Listed Companies

## Original Framework Context

Michael Porter's Five Forces framework (1979) assesses the competitive intensity and attractiveness of an industry from the perspective of an incumbent company. For Indian listed companies, a 6th force — **Government Protection / Policy** — is added because government policy plays an outsized role in many Indian sectors compared to purely free-market economies.

The goal: determine how durable the company's competitive position and margins are likely to be over the next 5-10 years.

---

## Force 1: Competitive Rivalry

**The question:** How intense is competition among existing players? Does rivalry destroy margins?

**Favorable conditions for the company:**
- 2-3 dominant players with rational pricing behavior
- Differentiated products where price is not the primary purchase decision
- Industry-wide growth absorbing all capacity (no need to fight for share)
- Strong brand loyalty among customers

**Unfavorable conditions:**
- Many players of similar size → chronic price wars
- Commodity product where price is the only differentiator
- Overcapacity → desperate pricing to fill plants
- Organized sector being disrupted by unorganized/informal competition

**India-specific considerations:**
- Many Indian industries have a large unorganized sector that competes on price without taxes/compliance costs (construction materials, food processing, textiles). GST has been formalizing some of these — check if the shift is ongoing or complete.
- Regional concentration matters: a company may dominate a state or region even if the national picture is competitive.

**Assessment questions:**
1. Who are the top 5 players and what are their approximate market shares?
2. Has there been a price war in the last 3 years?
3. Is the product commodity or differentiated?
4. Is the unorganized sector a competitive threat?

---

## Force 2: Threat of New Entrants

**The question:** How hard is it for a new player to enter and reach competitive scale?

**Favorable conditions (high barriers = favorable for incumbent):**
- Very high capital requirements to reach competitive scale (steel, cement, refining)
- Regulatory licensing required (banking, insurance, telecom spectrum, pharma APIs with USFDA/WHO-GMP)
- Established distribution network takes years to replicate (FMCG, building materials)
- Strong brand loyalty requiring years of advertising spend to overcome (premium FMCG, paints)
- Technology or IP barriers (specialty chemicals, pharma formulations with proprietary APIs)
- Long gestation and learning curve (defense, aerospace, specialty manufacturing)

**Unfavorable conditions:**
- Low capital requirements, easy-to-build technology
- No regulatory barriers — anyone can start
- Customers have low switching cost and are willing to try new brands
- No IP protection

**India-specific considerations:**
- **Private equity and venture capital** have been financing new entrants in consumer brands, fintech, and logistics at scale. If a sector is "hot," assume well-funded new entrants.
- **Chinese imports** have been a threat in solar, electronics, and some chemicals — check if government import duties have created effective protection.
- **Conglomerates entering new sectors** (Reliance, Tata, Adani) — any announcement of entry by these groups should be noted as a threat.

---

## Force 3: Threat of Substitutes

**The question:** Could customers switch to a fundamentally different product or service that serves the same need?

**Favorable conditions:**
- No viable substitute at comparable price/performance
- Substitute exists but switching cost is high (infrastructure lock-in, habit)
- Substitute timeline is distant (>10 years)

**Unfavorable conditions:**
- A technology change is underway that could make the category obsolete
- Substitute is already available at lower cost, just slower to adopt
- Customer preference is shifting (generational, lifestyle change)

**Examples relevant to portfolio sectors:**
- **Wires/cables (POLYCAB)**: Wireless power transmission is a substitute but commercially irrelevant at current technology levels. Near-term threat = minimal.
- **Two-wheelers (HEROMOTOCO)**: EVs are a substitute — not a new product but a propulsion substitution. Timeline: actively underway.
- **Paints (ASIANPAINT)**: Wallpaper and alternative wall finishes exist but low penetration in India. More relevant threat: distemper vs premium paint is a premiumization question rather than substitution.
- **QSR/food delivery (JUBLFOOD)**: Home delivery apps (Swiggy/Zomato) are a substitute channel, though Jubilant partly benefits from them too.

**How to assess timeline:**
- Imminent (<3 years): Active ongoing substitution visible in market data
- Medium-term (3-7 years): Substitute technology exists and is being scaled
- Long-term (>7 years): Substitute is theoretical or early-stage

## Force 4: Bargaining Power of Suppliers

**The question:** Can input suppliers raise prices without the company being able to push back or switch?

**Favorable conditions (low supplier power):**
- Many competing suppliers for the same input
- Input is a commodity traded on exchanges (copper, aluminum, natural gas, crude derivatives)
- Company can backward integrate or switch source with low cost
- Company is a large buyer representing significant % of supplier's revenue

**Unfavorable conditions (high supplier power):**
- Few specialized suppliers for a critical input (rare earth metals, specialty APIs, proprietary technology components)
- Supplier is a monopoly or near-monopoly (some APIs have only 2-3 global manufacturers)
- High switching cost (API pharma — changing supplier requires USFDA re-approval)
- Input price is globally determined, not negotiable (crude oil for petrochemicals)

**India-specific considerations:**
- Many Indian manufacturers depend on China for key inputs (APIs for pharma, panels for solar, components for electronics). This is both a supplier power issue and a geopolitical risk.
- Agricultural raw material dependent companies (FMCG with agri inputs) have seasonal supplier power fluctuations — monsoon/harvest cycle matters.
- Government-controlled inputs (natural gas for city gas distribution, transmission charges for power companies) — this overlaps with Force 7.

---

## Force 5: Bargaining Power of Buyers

**The question:** Can customers force prices down, demand better terms, or easily switch to competitors?

**Favorable conditions (low buyer power):**
- Fragmented retail buyers with no individual leverage (FMCG to consumers, retail jewellery)
- High switching cost for buyers (enterprise software, specialized equipment)
- Company's product is a small part of buyer's cost structure (not worth negotiating aggressively)
- Strong brand pull — consumers ask for the brand specifically

**Unfavorable conditions (high buyer power):**
- Few large institutional buyers representing significant % of company's revenue (government tenders, large infra companies)
- Buyers can easily switch between suppliers (commodity products)
- Buyer is price-sensitive and regularly runs auctions/tenders
- Buyer is consolidating (retail consolidation → modern trade negotiating leverage over FMCG brands)

**India-specific considerations:**
- **Government as buyer**: Companies dependent on government contracts (defense, infra, solar EPC) face high buyer power — tenders are competitive and payment timelines can be long.
- **Modern trade vs general trade**: In FMCG, Reliance Retail / D-Mart exert significant buying power vs traditional general trade. The mix matters.
- **Export revenue**: Buyers in developed markets (US pharma, EU chemicals) often have higher quality requirements and more leverage than domestic buyers.

## Force 6: Entry Barriers (Synthesized Assessment)

This is a synthesized verdict combining Capital, Regulatory, Brand, and Distribution barriers.

**How to classify:**

| Barrier Type | Examples in Indian Context |
|-------------|---------------------------|
| Capital | Steel, cement, refineries, semiconductor fab (billions needed) |
| Regulatory | Banking license, insurance license, USFDA plant approval, telecom spectrum |
| Brand | HUL, Asian Paints, Nestle — decades of advertising → top-of-mind recall |
| Distribution | FMCG companies with 1M+ retail touchpoints, rural distribution depth |
| Technology/IP | Specialty chemicals with proprietary processes, pharma with complex generics |
| Location/Resource | Mines, ports, prime retail locations, water rights |

**Overall Entry Barrier:**
- **Strong** (2+ types of barrier present): Favorable for incumbent — new entrant faces multi-dimensional challenge
- **Moderate** (1 barrier type): Neutral — possible to enter but takes time
- **Weak** (no significant barriers): Unfavorable — fragile competitive position

## Force 7: Government Protection / Policy (India-Specific)

**This force has no equivalent in original Porter's framework and is specific to the Indian investment context.**

The Indian government actively shapes competitive dynamics through:

**Types of Protection/Tailwinds:**
- **PLI Schemes** (Production Linked Incentive): Government pays incentives for domestic production in sectors like solar, EV, pharma, specialty chemicals, electronics. Direct margin enhancer for eligible companies.
- **Import Duties**: High customs duty on imports makes foreign competition expensive (e.g., wires/cables, FMCG goods, some chemicals). Protects domestic players.
- **Mandatory Government Procurement**: Defense (DRDO/HAL), railways, ONGC — companies with government relationships benefit from captive demand.
- **Make-in-India / Aatmanirbhar Bharat**: Policy push to substitute imports domestically — explicit tailwind for domestic manufacturers of electronics, defense, chemicals, medical devices.
- **Infrastructure Push**: Government spending on highways, railways, power — creates demand for construction materials, cables, industrial equipment.
- **Sector-Specific Regulation as Moat**: RBI's stringent bank/NBFC licensing effectively limits new competition in financial services.

**Types of Government Risk (Unfavorable):**
- **Price controls**: Pharma essential medicines have DPCO price caps; city gas distribution has regulated tariffs.
- **GST / Tax regime changes**: Sudden GST rate changes can disrupt pricing structure (sin tax on tobacco, cement GST revision history).
- **Government as dominant buyer** (covered in Force 5): Tender-based revenue with potential delays.
- **Regulatory mandate costs**: Environment compliance, EV transition mandates for auto companies — cost without immediate revenue.

**Assessment:**
- Net positive: PLI eligibility + import duty protection + Make-in-India beneficiary → 🟢
- Neutral / mixed: Some protection, some risk → 🟡
- Net negative: Price controls + government payment delays + mandatory compliance costs without offsetting benefit → 🔴

## Putting It Together: Industry Attractiveness Score

After assessing all 7 forces, assign each as Favorable / Neutral / Unfavorable.

**Interpretation guide:**
- 5-7 Favorable: Very attractive industry structure for this company
- 3-4 Favorable: Average attractiveness — execution matters more than industry tailwinds
- 0-2 Favorable: Structurally challenged industry — requires exceptional moat or turnaround story

**Important:** The forces analysis is for the **industry**, not the company. A company in an unattractive industry can still be a great investment if it has an exceptional moat within that industry. Conversely, a weak company in an attractive industry may underperform. Phase 2 financial analysis + company-specific moat analysis (Phase 3) will complete the picture.
