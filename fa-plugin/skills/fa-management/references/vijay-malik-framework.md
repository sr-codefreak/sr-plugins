# Dr. Vijay Malik's Management Analysis Framework

## About Dr. Vijay Malik

Dr. Vijay Malik is an independent equity analyst and value investor based in India. His blog (drvijaymalik.com) is one of the most detailed publicly available resources for management integrity analysis of Indian listed companies. His framework focuses on identifying management red flags **before** doing any financial analysis, on the premise that bad management can make even great businesses uninvestable.

Key principle: "A business run by dishonest management will eventually destroy shareholder value regardless of how good the underlying business is."

---

## The 10-Point Integrity Checklist — Detailed Guide

### 1. Management Remuneration (<5% of PAT)

**What to check:** Total director remuneration (sitting fees + salary + commission + perquisites) as a percentage of Profit After Tax (PAT).

**How to find it:**

- Annual Report → Directors' Report → section on "Remuneration Policy" or "Managerial Remuneration"
- Annual Report → Notes to Accounts → Note on "Key Management Personnel Compensation"
- BSE XBRL filings: search bseindia.com → company page → Annual Reports
- Screener.in → company page shows "Salary of promoters" in financial data for some companies

**Thresholds:**

- <5% PAT → 🟢 Green
- 5–10% PAT → 🟡 Amber (explain context — e.g., PAT is temporarily low due to investment cycle)
- > 10% PAT → 🔴 Red
- Family members on payroll with high remuneration and vague roles → 🔴 Red regardless of total %

**Why this matters:** Excessive management remuneration is a direct wealth transfer from shareholders to promoters. It often indicates the promoter views the company as a personal income source rather than a business to build for all shareholders.

**Nuance:** A company in a growth phase with temporarily depressed PAT may show a high % — use judgment. Look at the absolute amount and the trend over 3-5 years.

---

### 2. Family Member Salaries

**What to check:** Are promoter family members on the company payroll? If yes, are their roles clearly defined and experience-relevant?

**How to find it:**

- Annual Report → Corporate Governance Report → "Related Party Disclosures" → salaries to KMP relatives
- Annual Report → Note 33/34 (Related Party Transactions) → Key Management Personnel list

**Red flags:**

- Spouse, children, siblings, in-laws on payroll with vague designations (e.g., "Director" with no functional role described)
- Compensation for family members grossly disproportionate to their apparent responsibilities
- Multiple family members extracting significant salaries simultaneously

**Green flags:**

- Family members in relevant executive roles with clear functional responsibilities
- Second-generation family members with traceable industry experience before joining

---

### 3. Related Party Transactions (RPT) — No Abnormalities

**What to check:** All transactions between the listed company and promoter-related entities. Look for size, pricing, and trend.

**How to find it:**

- Annual Report → Notes to Accounts → Note titled "Related Party Transactions" (usually Note 33–38)
- Check: purchases from promoter entities, sales to promoter entities, loans given/taken, rent paid, services rendered

**Red flags:**

- RPT as % of revenue growing faster than standalone revenue (promoter entities capturing company's growth)
- Inter-company loans at non-market (below-market or above-market) interest rates
- Goods/services purchased from promoter entities at above-market prices
- Goods/services sold to promoter entities at below-market prices
- Unexplained increase in RPT quantum year-on-year
- Loans/advances to promoter entities without clear business purpose

**Green flags:**

- RPT declining as % of revenue over time (company becoming more independent)
- RPT at clearly documented market rates with audit committee approval
- RPT limited to genuine business necessities (e.g., trademark license fees at standard rates)

**Analytical approach:** Compare RPT/Revenue ratio over 5 years. A stable or declining ratio is healthy; a rising ratio needs scrutiny.

---

### 4. Promoter Criminal Record — SEBI/SFIO/ED/CBI Actions

**What to check:** Any regulatory enforcement action, criminal proceedings, or fraud investigations involving the promoter or company.

**How to find it:**

- SEBI enforcement orders: sebi.gov.in → Enforcement → Orders (search by company/person name)
- MCA company database: mca.gov.in (compounding orders, prosecution notices)
- News search: "[Promoter Name] SEBI" / "[Company] SFIO" / "[Company] ED"
- Sujata Dalal / Moneylife: site:moneylife.in "[Company Name]" (most comprehensive fraud watchlist for Indian listed companies)

**Hard Red flags (🔴, non-negotiable):**

- SEBI enforcement order against promoter (debarment, penalty, disgorgement)
- SFIO (Serious Fraud Investigation Office) investigation
- ED (Enforcement Directorate) proceedings
- CBI/police criminal cases related to financial fraud
- On Sujata Dalal's watch list at Moneylife

**Soft flags (🟡):**

- Past minor SEBI notices that were resolved with small penalty and no admission of guilt
- Promoter associated with a previously failed/delisted company (not fraud, but track record concern)

**Note:** Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, especially for small-cap companies with less media coverage. Note data limitations explicitly.

---

### 5. Media Appearances — Sober and Followed-Up

**What to check:** Frequency and quality of management media appearances. Do they follow through on public statements?

**How to find it:**

- News archives: Economic Times, Business Standard, Moneycontrol, CNBC TV18 interviews
- Search: "[Company Name] [Promoter Name] interview" for last 2 years
- YouTube: search company name for investor day / AGM recordings

**Red flags:**

- Frequent TV appearances with bullish price targets or "story" narratives
- Statements about new orders, products, markets that never materialize
- Management that is visible during bull runs in the stock and silent during bad periods
- Promotional language: "we will be the next [famous company]" type statements

**Green flags:**

- Infrequent, sober, fact-based investor communications
- AGM and analyst call transcripts that show consistent guidance vs actuals
- Management that proactively informs about challenges, not just opportunities
- Announcements that are specific and verifiable, with follow-up updates

---

### 6. Share-Price Focus — No Price-Pumping Statements

**What to check:** Does management make statements specifically referencing share price appreciation or that appear timed to influence stock price?

**How to find it:**

- Same sources as criterion 5 (news, interviews)
- BSE/NSE corporate announcements: check for press releases about "company's strong prospects" during market declines

**Red flags:**

- "The stock is undervalued and will reach ₹X" type statements by management
- Announcements of MOUs, JVs, or contracts that are vague and never progress
- Positive news releases timed suspiciously around stock price declines or promoter buying windows

**Green flags:**

- Management focuses discussions on operational KPIs, not stock price
- Consistently avoids commenting on valuation in public forums

---

### 7. CFO and Auditor Resignations

**What to check:** Have the CFO or statutory auditor changed in the last 5 years? If yes, why?

**How to find it:**

- BSE/NSE corporate announcements: search company → "Board Meeting Outcome" filings for change in CFO/auditor
- Annual Report → Auditor's Report (if auditor changed, new auditor will be named)
- News search: "[Company] CFO resigned" / "[Company] auditor change"

**Classification:**

- Auditor rotation under SEBI/Companies Act mandate → ⬜ N/A (mandatory, not a red flag)
- CFO retirement after long tenure → 🟡 (note but not concerning)
- CFO resignation without stated reason or with vague reason → 🟡 to 🔴 depending on context
- Multiple CFO changes in 3 years → 🔴
- Auditor resignation mid-term (not at year end) with vague reason → 🔴
- Auditor replaced with a lesser-known/local firm after Big4 → 🟡

**Why this matters:** CFOs and auditors who resign often do so because they are uncomfortable signing off on the company's accounts or management behavior. These departures are early warning signals.

---

### 8. Auditor Fees — Proportionate to Company Size

**What to check:** Are audit fees (statutory audit + tax audit + other certifications) reasonable relative to the company's size and complexity?

**How to find it:**

- Annual Report → Notes to Accounts → Note on "Auditor Remuneration" or "Payments to Auditors"
- Compare to peers of similar revenue size

**Red flags:**

- Abnormally high audit fees for company size → may indicate auditor is being "paid off" to approve questionable items
- Audit fees that jump significantly without obvious reason (new Big4 firm, major regulatory change)
- Very low audit fees for a complex company → may indicate insufficient audit scrutiny

**Benchmark (approximate):**

- Revenue <₹500 cr: audit fees typically ₹25-75 lakhs
- Revenue ₹500-2000 cr: audit fees typically ₹50-150 lakhs
- Revenue >₹2000 cr: audit fees typically ₹1-5 cr
These are rough guides — complexity, industry, and auditor tier matter significantly.

---

### 9. Egoistic Management — Accepts Mistakes

**What to check:** Does management acknowledge when plans don't work out? Do they explain corrective actions? Or do they always have external reasons for underperformance?

**How to find it:**

- Annual Report → MD&A (Management Discussion & Analysis) section — compare across 3-5 years
- AGM transcripts or Investor Day presentations — tone and responses to analyst questions
- Compare guidance given in Year N's AR vs actual results in Year N+1's AR

**Red flags:**

- Every year's AR blames macro factors (inflation, rupee, raw material costs) without any self-reflection
- Management targets consistently unmet without explanation in the next AR
- Defensive or dismissive responses to analyst questions in earnings calls
- The word "transformational" or "paradigm shift" used repeatedly without substance following

**Green flags:**

- AR explicitly acknowledges a specific initiative that did not work and explains why
- Guidance is calibrated and conservative — management under-promises and over-delivers
- Analysts can access management and receive direct answers (not just PR-speak)

---

### 10. Promoter Pledging

**What to check:** What percentage of promoter holdings are pledged as collateral? Is the trend worsening?

**How to find it:**

- NSE/BSE shareholding pattern (quarterly filing): shows pledged shares
- Screener.in → company page → shareholding tab → "Promoter Pledging"
- News search for recent pledging events

**Thresholds:**

- 0% pledging → 🟢
- 1–25% pledging → 🟢 (acceptable, common for working capital needs)
- 25–50% pledging → 🟡 (monitor — why is this level sustained?)
- 50–80% pledging → 🔴 (serious concern — stock price risk)
- > 80% pledging → 🔴 (critical — any stock decline could force lender to sell)

**Additional red flags:**

- Pledging trend increasing quarter over quarter
- Pledging without stated purpose
- Pledging during a period when the company is also paying high dividends (extracting cash while pledging equity)

## Important Caveats

1. **This analysis is a filter, not a verdict.** Flags indicate areas needing deeper investigation, not automatic disqualification.
2. **Context matters.** A company with one amber flag in an otherwise clean record is very different from one with multiple amber flags.
3. **Data for small-caps is limited.** Missing data ≠ clean record. Note unavailable data points explicitly.
4. **Dr. Vijay Malik's blog** has detailed case studies of companies with management red flags. Reviewing his analysis methodology at drvijaymalik.com for specific sectors or similar companies provides additional context.
